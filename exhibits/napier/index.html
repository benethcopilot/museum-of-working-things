Numbers & Elements · Edinburgh, 1617

Napier’s Bones

The same Scottish laird who invented logarithms made a set of ivory rods that turn long multiplication into adding small numbers. Lay the rods side by side, pick a row, and add along the diagonals.

Add rods from the box above, or click a rod on the board to take it off. Click a number on the left-hand rod to read that row.

The reckoning

Why the diagonals work

Each square holds a product from the times table, split into tens above the slanted line and units below it. Reading along a row, the units of one rod and the tens of the next rod belong to the same place value, so the slanted strips line them up for you to add.

Bigger multipliers

To multiply by 913, read row 9, row 1 and row 3 separately, shift each one by its place, and add the three lines. You only ever need the times table up to 9, and the rods have already done that part.

Four tables on every rod

Napier’s rods were square, made of metal, wood or ivory, with a different times table engraved on each of the four faces. A small set could make any number by turning the rods to the faces needed.

From the laird of Merchiston

  1. 1614

    John Napier of Merchiston, near Edinburgh, publishes his logarithms. The bones are a separate invention, and a much simpler one.

  2. 1617

    His book Rabdologiae describes the rods. He coined the word rabdology, counting with rods. It is printed in Edinburgh the year he dies.

  3. 1623

    Wilhelm Schickard in Germany designs a calculating machine that builds rotating Napier’s rods into an adding mechanism.

  4. 1891

    Henri Genaille invents a version, later called the Genaille–Lucas rulers, that shows the carries with printed arrows, so you can read the answer without adding at all.

Adding instead of multiplying

The bones, the logarithms and the slide rule all do the same trick: they turn multiplying, which is slow and error-prone, into adding, which almost anyone can do reliably. That idea runs through four centuries of calculating machines.

Napier’s bones also do division, by finding the largest row that fits, and with an extra rod they can extract square roots.
