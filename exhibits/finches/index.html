Darwin’s Finches

On a tiny volcanic island, Peter and Rosemary Grant measured the beak of every medium ground finch they could catch. Then a drought came, and they watched natural selection happen in a single year. Run the seasons on their island and see the beaks change.

Evolution you can watch

  1. 1835

    Charles Darwin visits the Galápagos on HMS Beagle and collects finches, though he doesn’t yet see how much their beaks tell.

  2. 1973

    Peter and Rosemary Grant begin studying the finches of Daphne Major, catching, measuring and banding nearly every bird year after year.

  3. 1977

    Almost no rain falls. Small, soft seeds run out. What remains are the hard, spiny fruits of caltrop, which only big, deep-beaked birds can crack. Most of the medium ground finches die.

  4. 1981

    Peter Boag and Peter Grant publish the result in Science: the survivors were not a random sample, and the selection was the strongest yet measured in a wild vertebrate.

  5. 1983

    A strong El Niño brings floods of rain and plenty of small seeds. Now smaller birds do better, and the trend swings back.

Two things have to be true

Natural selection only changes the next generation if birds differ in a trait that matters for survival, and if that trait is passed from parents to chicks. Beak depth is both: it decides which seeds a finch can open, and big-beaked parents have big-beaked young.

Nothing in a bird’s life made its beak grow during the drought. The change came from which birds lived to breed.
