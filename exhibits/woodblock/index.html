Making & Mending · Edo, Japan · 1831

Edo Woodblock Print

A Japanese colour print was made by four trades. The artist drew it, a carver cut a separate cherry-wood block for every colour, and the printer laid one damp sheet on each block in turn, rubbing it with a flat pad called a baren. Two small notches cut into every block, the kentō, put the paper in exactly the same place each time. You are the printer. Seven blocks are carved and waiting.

One block for every colour

The carver pasted the artist’s drawing face down on a cherry block and cut away everything but the black lines: the key block. Proofs from it went back to the artist, who marked one colour on each, and each became another block. Here are this print’s seven, with the colour each one carries. Every block is carved in reverse, like a seal, so the print comes out the right way round: look at the title. Where two colours overlap, the thin water-based inks mix on the paper: the pine trunk is printed in both yellow and pink.

Bokashi: a fade by hand

The sky and the sea in this print are bokashi. The printer wiped the block with a damp cloth, loaded colour along one edge of a wide brush and drew it across, so the ink bled into the wet wood and faded out. This straight-line fade, ichimonji bokashi, is the one Hokusai and Hiroshige used for skies, seas and horizons. It had to be brushed again for every single sheet, so no two impressions match exactly. Set the fade on blocks 6 and 7 to make yours.

The edge of a fade is never perfectly even. Printers called a straight fade with a ragged edge ichimonji-mura-bokashi, and you will see that unevenness in your print too.

The blue revolution

Earlier printers had only plant blues that faded in the light. Prussian blue, a synthetic pigment the Japanese called bero-ai, “Berlin indigo”, was strong and lasting. In January 1831 Hokusai’s publisher Nishimuraya Yohachi advertised the new blue in his series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, and its first ten prints, including The Great Wave off Kanagawa, are among the first Japanese prints to use it.

As the blocks wore, the pink and yellow of the sky faded first in later copies, and collectors still use that wear to tell early impressions from late ones.

Four trades, one print

  • PublisherCommissioned the design, paid for the blocks, sold the prints. Owning the blocks was the nearest thing to copyright.
  • ArtistDrew the design in ink on thin paper and chose the colours.
  • CarverPasted the drawing to cherry wood and cut it, destroying the original drawing as the block took shape.
  • PrinterBrushed water-based colour onto each block, laid the paper in the kentō and rubbed it with the baren.

Usually only the artist and the publisher were named on the finished print. The baren itself is a coil of twisted bamboo-leaf rope in a disc of laminated paper, wrapped in a bamboo leaf.

  1. 770

    The Hyakumantō Darani: Buddhist charms printed from blocks, the start of woodblock printing in Japan.

  2. 1670s

    Hishikawa Moronobu’s black-ink prints launch ukiyo-e, pictures of the “floating world”.

  3. 1740s

    Okumura Masanobu and others print areas of colour from several blocks.

  4. 1760s

    Suzuki Harunobu’s “brocade pictures”, nishiki-e, use up to a dozen blocks.

  5. 1831

    Hokusai’s The Great Wave off Kanagawa, printed in Prussian blue.

  6. 1856

    Hiroshige begins One Hundred Famous Views of Edo, full of bokashi sunrises; he dies in 1858.

  7. 1915

    Publisher Watanabe Shōzaburō names the shin-hanga, “new prints”, reviving the old craft.
