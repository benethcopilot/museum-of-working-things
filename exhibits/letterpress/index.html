Letterpress Type Case

Before computers, every letter in a printed page was a small block of metal, picked by hand from a case like this one. Set a line, space it until it fits the stick exactly, then ink it and pull a print.

The California job case

89 compartments. Small letters are on the left, sized by how often each letter is used, so the e box is the biggest. Capitals run A to Z on the right, except J and U, which early English printers did not use, so they come after Z. The numbers in each box count the pieces left.

Composing stick

Type on your keyboard or tap the case. Each piece goes in left to right, nick up, so the line looks upside down. That is how compositors saw it.

Galley and press

Finished lines wait here in a frame called a chase, with a thin strip of lead between them. Type is a mirror image, so it reads backwards here and right way round on paper.

When the printing is done, compositors put every piece back in its own box. That job was called distributing the type.

How a compositor set a page

  1. Set the stickPick pieces of type, called sorts, from the case one at a time and drop them into the composing stick, left to right, nick up.
  2. Space the wordsA 3-em space, a third as wide as the type is tall, goes between words. Spaces are lower than the letters, so they never print.
  3. JustifyEach line must fill the stick exactly, or it falls apart when you lift it. Add spaces between words, or quads at the ends, until the last piece slides in snugly.
  4. Make up the pageSlide the lines onto a galley with a thin strip of lead between them, so the lines don’t crowd.
  5. Lock up and printWedge the type tight in an iron chase, roll ink over it with a brayer, lay paper on top and press.
  6. DistributePut every sort back in its own box, ready for the next job.

Words from the type case

True

Upper case, lower case

Before the combined case, printers kept capitals and small letters in two separate cases on a sloping stand. The capitals sat in the upper case and the small letters in the lower case.

True

Leading

Thin strips of lead went between lines of type to space them apart. Designers still call the space between lines leading, said like the metal.

True

Font

A font was a complete set of one size and style of type, sold with more of the common letters than the rare ones. That is why this case can run out of e’s.

Probably not

Out of sorts

A sort is one piece of type, and printers did run short. But the phrase turns up in 1562, about a century before printers called their type sorts, so the printing story is probably not where it came from.

Probably not

Mind your p’s and q’s

In metal type a small p and q are easy to mix up, so the printing story sounds right. The earliest known uses don’t mention printing at all, and nobody knows for sure where the phrase came from.

Before Gutenberg

Movable type began in China, where Bi Sheng made letters of baked clay around the 1040s. In Korea, a Buddhist book called the Jikji was printed with metal type in 1377.

Gutenberg’s shop

Around 1450 in Mainz, Johannes Gutenberg combined metal type cast in a hand mould, an oil-based ink and a screw press. His Bible followed a few years later, and printing spread across Europe within decades.

The California case

J. L. Ringwalt described this layout in 1871, as San Francisco printers used it. Putting both cases in one tray was claimed to save a compositor more than half a mile of hand travel a day. Experienced typesetters could read what someone was setting just by watching which boxes their hand went to.
