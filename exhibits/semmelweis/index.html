Semmelweis’s Two Clinics

Vienna’s great hospital had two maternity clinics under one roof. In the early 1840s about one mother in ten died of childbed fever in the first, and about one in twenty-five in the second. Ignaz Semmelweis, a young doctor in the first clinic, set out to learn why using the hospital’s own records. Open them and work through the problem as he did.

Before the autopsies

To make his case, Semmelweis went back to 1784. Until 1822 Vienna’s maternity wards were run by Johann Lucas Boër. In 1823 Johann Klein took over and taught obstetrics through anatomy, so students learned from autopsies. Dublin’s Rotunda Hospital, which Semmelweis used for comparison, stood apart from any general hospital, and its students did no autopsies.

Vienna’s rate jumped the year the autopsies began, while Dublin’s barely moved. Semmelweis saw the same pattern as in his two clinics: where doctors handled the dead, mothers died.

Right, and ignored

Semmelweis could show that washing worked, but not why. Germs had not been discovered, and many doctors were offended at being told their hands carried death. He chose chlorinated lime because it worked best at getting the smell of the autopsy room off the hands. He did not know it was killing bacteria.

His post in Vienna was not renewed in 1849. In Pest he brought childbed fever almost to an end at the small Szent Rókus Hospital: 8 deaths in 933 births from 1851 to 1855. When he finally published his book in 1861, most leading doctors rejected it. His successor in Vienna, Carl Braun, wrote a textbook listing thirty causes of childbed fever; infection from corpses came twenty-eighth.

Within twenty years of his death, Louis Pasteur, Joseph Lister and Robert Koch had shown that germs cause disease. Semmelweis is now called the saviour of mothers, and the university of medicine in Budapest bears his name.

  1. 1818

    Ignaz Semmelweis is born in Buda, Hungary, on 1 July.

  2. 1846

    He becomes assistant in the First Clinic in July. A government commission blames rough examinations by students.

  3. 1847

    Jakob Kolletschka dies in March after an autopsy wound. In mid-May Semmelweis has everyone wash in chlorinated lime before examining.

  4. 1848

    The First Clinic’s rate for the year is 1.3%, no higher than the midwives’.

  5. 1849

    His appointment ends in March. He moves to Pest the next year.

  6. 1851

    Head of obstetrics at Szent Rókus Hospital in Pest, where childbed fever almost disappears.

  7. 1861

    He publishes The Etiology, Concept and Prophylaxis of Childbed Fever.

  8. 1865

    He dies on 13 August in an asylum in Vienna, of blood poisoning, aged 47.
