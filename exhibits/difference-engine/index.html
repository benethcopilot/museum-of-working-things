Difference Engine

Printed tables of numbers ran navigation, banking and engineering, and they were full of mistakes. Charles Babbage designed a machine that could make them by turning a handle, using nothing but addition. Set it up, turn the crank, and watch the carries ripple up the wheels.

    Why addition is enough

    Write down a table of a polynomial, subtract each number from the next, then do the same to the differences. Sooner or later a column comes out the same all the way down.

    For a polynomial with x² as its biggest term, the second differences never change. For x³, the third differences never change. That constant column is the key.

    Run the subtraction backwards and every number in the table can be built by adding. Set the top row on the engine’s columns: the first value, the first difference, and so on. Each turn of the handle adds every column into the one on its left, and out comes the next line of the table.

    The engine never multiplies, and it never makes a slip of the pen.

    Catch the misprint

    Many errors in old tables came from the typesetter, not the calculator: two digits swapped, one digit wrong. Differences catch them. Find the wrong line in this table.

    A machine that took 170 years

    1. 1822

      Babbage announces his difference engine in a paper to the Astronomical Society. With John Herschel he had been asked to check tables for the Nautical Almanac and found the hand-made ones disagreeing.

    2. 1823

      The government grants £1,700 to build a full-size engine. The design grows to some 25,000 precision parts.

    3. 1832

      Babbage and his engineer Joseph Clement finish a working one-seventh section: six-digit numbers and second differences.

    4. 1833

      Seventeen-year-old Ada Byron, later Ada Lovelace, meets Babbage in June and that month sees the working section turn.

    5. 1842

      After more than £17,000 and no finished engine, the government gives up. Babbage has moved on to his Analytical Engine.

    6. 1853

      In Sweden, Per Georg Scheutz and his son Edvard finish a working difference engine inspired by Babbage, with 15-digit numbers and fourth differences.

    7. 1991

      London’s Science Museum completes Babbage’s Difference Engine No. 2, designed in 1846–49, for the 200th anniversary of his birth. It works. Its printer follows in 2002.

    Errors by steam

    Babbage reckoned that many mistakes in published tables were not in the arithmetic at all but in copying and typesetting. So his designs included a printer that pressed the results straight into soft material to make printing plates, with no person in between.

    Difference Engine No. 2 works on 31-digit numbers with seventh differences. This one has four columns of seven wheels, enough for cubes.
