Hammond Organ

Inside a Hammond, ninety-one steel wheels spin past magnets, and each one hums a single pure tone. Nine drawbars mix those tones into one sound. Pull them, play, and hear how a church organ, a jazz trio and a rock band all came out of the same box.

What you are hearing

Each bar is one drawbar’s tone, placed by how many times the 8′ pitch it vibrates.

Add the pure tones together and you get this wave shape, repeating hundreds of times a second.

Match the sound by ear

The organ hides a registration. Listen to it, set the drawbars until yours sounds the same, then check. A tick or a cross appears under each drawbar. Organists learned to do this by ear, and after a while you can hear a 4′ or a 2⅔′ sitting inside a sound.

Ninety-one wheels on one shaft

A synchronous motor locked to the 60-cycle mains turns a long shaft at exactly 1,200 revolutions a minute: 20 turns a second. Twelve pairs of gears step that speed up or down, one pair for each note of the scale, and each gear drives small steel tonewheels with 2 to 192 bumps on their rims. Every bump that passes a magnet makes one cycle of the tone.

Gears only come in whole numbers of teeth, so Hammond chose pairs that land very close to the right pitch. The harmonic drawbars borrow those same wheels, which means the 1⅗′ drawbar is about 14 cents sharp of the true fifth harmonic. That slight sourness is part of the Hammond sound.

From clocks to jazz clubs

Laurens Hammond was an engineer who made his money on electric clocks driven by synchronous motors, the same kind of motor that would keep his organ in tune. With John Hanert he patented an electric organ in 1934 and sold it to churches as a cheaper alternative to a pipe organ.

Jazz players took it into clubs instead. Jimmy Smith’s registration of 88 8000 000 became the classic organ-trio sound, and the B-3 of 1954, with its harmonic percussion, became the most famous Hammond of all. Most players ran it through a Leslie speaker, whose spinning horn and drum bend the sound with the Doppler effect. Don Leslie offered his invention to Hammond, who turned it down; Hammond bought the Leslie company in 1980.

  1. 1928

    Laurens Hammond starts the Hammond Clock Company.

  2. 1934

    Hammond and John Hanert patent an “electrical musical instrument”.

  3. 1935

    The Model A goes on sale in June for $1,193, with two 61-note manuals and a pedalboard.

  4. 1936

    The Model BC adds a second set of tonewheels, slightly sharp and flat, to thicken the sound.

  5. 1954

    The B-3 and C-3 add harmonic percussion. The B-3 stays in production until 1974.

  6. 1975

    Hammond stops making tonewheel organs and switches to electronics.

  7. 1985

    The Hammond Organ Company goes out of business. Suzuki buys the name in 1989 and builds digital Hammonds today.

The sound here is synthesized: nine sine waves per key, tuned to the tonewheel frequencies, with a simulated Leslie.
