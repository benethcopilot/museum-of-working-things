Mendeleev’s Table

In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev sorted the known elements by weight and by how they behave, and the story goes that he laid them out like cards in a game of solitaire. Where no element fit, he left a gap and said one nobody had found yet belonged there. Deal his cards and find the gaps yourself.

Predict the missing elements

Mendeleev estimated each missing element from its neighbors, since weights rise steadily along a series. Set your guess for its atomic weight, then see what was found.

The prediction that convinced everyone

Mendeleev named each missing element after a known one in its group, adding the Sanskrit prefix eka-, “one”. In 1871 he described eka-silicon in detail. In 1886 Clemens Winkler found germanium in a silver ore and measured it.

Measure it again

When Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran found gallium in 1875, he measured its density as 4.7. Mendeleev had predicted 5.9 for eka-aluminium, and he wrote to suggest the measurement be checked. With a purer sample it came out at 5.9.

Trust the pattern

Tellurium weighs a little more than iodine, yet it behaves like sulfur and selenium while iodine behaves like chlorine and bromine. Mendeleev put them in the order their chemistry asked for. In 1913 Henry Moseley showed why: elements line up by atomic number, the charge on the nucleus, not by weight.

A textbook needs an order

Mendeleev was writing a chemistry textbook in St. Petersburg and wanted a sensible order for its chapters. Sorting the 63 elements known at the time, he found that their properties repeat. He published his first table in 1869 and the improved one this page uses in 1871. The game deals its first seven series; the full table went on to series 12, through gold, mercury, lead and uranium.

The gaps fill in

Gallium came in 1875 and scandium in 1879, which Per Teodor Cleve recognized as eka-boron. Germanium followed in 1886. The gap under manganese waited until 1937, when Carlo Perrier and Emilio Segrè made technetium from molybdenum that had been bombarded in a cyclotron. Its name comes from the Greek for “artificial”.

What he didn’t see coming

In 1894 Lord Rayleigh and William Ramsay found argon, a gas that reacts with almost nothing. Ramsay went on to find helium on Earth in 1895, and with Morris Travers, neon, krypton and xenon in 1898. They needed a whole new column, and the table took it in without breaking.
