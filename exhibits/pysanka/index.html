Pysanka Written Eggs

A pysanka is not painted. Its name comes from pysaty, “to write”, because the design is written onto the shell in hot beeswax. Wherever the wax goes, the colour underneath is kept. Dip the egg in a dye, write again, dip again, and only at the very end warm the egg and wipe the wax away to find out what you made.

Drag on the egg to write with wax. Switch to Turn to roll it round.

Dye jars, light to dark

    Light to dark, layer by layer

    1. Write on white

      Beeswax was warmed in a little bowl on the family stove. The stylus, a scrap of thin brass rolled into a cone round a needle and tied to a willow stick, writes it onto a raw white egg.

    2. Dye and write again

      The egg goes into the lightest dye. Now wax covers what should stay that colour, and so on, jar after jar. Plant dyes could take hours, so eggs were written in batches.

    3. Darkest last

      The order was always light to dark: yellow, orange, red, purple, black. A yellow bath barely shows on an egg that is already red.

    4. Wipe it off

      The egg was warmed in the stove, or dipped for a moment in boiling water, and the melted wax wiped gently away. Every hidden colour appears at once.

    Written, dotted, scratched

    Pysanky

    Raw eggs written in wax with a stylus, the full wax-resist method, using traditional folk motifs.

    Krapanky

    “Dotted”: wax dripped from a beeswax candle or daubed on with a stick, leaving spots.

    Drop-pull eggs

    In the Lemko and Boiko lands, a pinhead dipped in wax: a drop, then a pull, makes tears and commas.

    Krashanky

    Boiled eggs dyed one colour, often red from onion skins, to be blessed and eaten at Easter.

    Driapanky

    “Scratched”: a dyed egg with the pattern scratched through to the white shell.

    Fragile and very old

    Eggshells rarely survive in the ground, so the oldest pysanky are recent finds: a goose egg with a wave pattern, found in 2013 in a rainwater system in Lviv dating to the 15th or 16th century, and a crushed egg from the end of the 17th century, excavated at Baturyn in 2008. The idea is far older. Before Christianity, many Slavs worshipped a sun god, Dazhboh, and eggs written with signs of nature were spring talismans: the earth reborn after winter, as life bursts from an egg. In Kievan Rus’, potters made glazed clay eggs, and more than seventy have been dug up.

    The Hutsuls of the Carpathian mountains say the fate of the world depends on the pysanka. A horrible serpent lies chained to a cliff, and every year he sends his servants out to count the eggs. If few were written, his chains loosen and he roams the earth; if there are more, the chains are pulled tighter for another year.
