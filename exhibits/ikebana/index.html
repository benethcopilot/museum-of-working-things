Ikebana Moribana

Ikebana is more than flowers in a vase. It is built from a few stems set at measured lengths and angles, with the empty space between them as carefully placed as the flowers. In moribana, “piled-up flowers”, the stems stand on a kenzan, a heavy bed of needles, in a shallow dish of water. Set the three main stems in the upright form, then fill it in.

    Three stems and a triangle

    Many arrangements are built on a triangle with three unequal sides, marked by three main stems. Some schools call them shin, soe and hikae, and give them meanings: heaven, human and earth, an idea credited to the painter Sōami in the 15th century. The rest of the material fills in inside that triangle, and the space left empty matters as much as what is there.

    In the upright form of moribana, the primary stem stands straight up and is about as long as the dish is wide and deep together. The secondary stem is about two-thirds as long, leaning 45° toward the front left. The ornamental stem is about half as long, leaning 60° toward the front right. Seen from above, the three tips make a right triangle.

    The water shows the season. In spring and summer more water is left open at the front, which is why the kenzan moves back in the spring setting here.

    Ways of arranging

    Rikka, “standing flowers”

    The oldest formal style: a Buddhist picture of a whole landscape, built from nine main branches.

    Nageire, “thrown-in”

    A freer style in a tall vase, growing out of the simple flowers of the tea room.

    Seika or shōka, “pure flowers”

    Only three main parts, to show the beauty of one kind of plant.

    Moribana, “piled-up flowers”

    A shallow dish and a kenzan, introduced by Ohara Unshin around 1890, so arrangements could stand in Western-style rooms as well as the alcove.

    Jiyūka, “free flowers”

    Any material at all, the 20th century’s modern style.

    From altar offerings to an art

    Flowers were first placed on Buddhist altars as offerings. The Ikenobō school, the oldest, traces itself to priests at a temple beside a pond, ike, in Kyoto, and its formal styles began in the mid-15th century, when the shōgun Ashikaga Yoshimasa championed tea, painting and flower arranging. Each school is still led by an iemoto, often handed down within a family.

    1. 600s

      The envoy Ono no Imoko brings the custom of Buddhist altar flowers from China.

    2. 1400s

      The first systematised styles, including rikka, take shape among Ikenobō priests; Sōami imagines the three elements.

    3. 1500s

      Nageire gains favour alongside the tea ceremony.

    4. 1890s

      Ohara Unshin introduces moribana; the Ohara school is founded in 1895.

    5. 1927

      Teshigahara Sōfu founds the Sōgetsu school, the best known modern school.
