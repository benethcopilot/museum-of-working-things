A thousand cranes

Orizuru means “folded crane”: ori, folded, and tsuru, crane. Cranes were said to live a thousand years, and paper cranes were offered at shrines and temples by people asking for blessings. A string of a thousand is senbazuru, and folding one is said to grant a wish.

Sadako Sasaki was two when the atomic bomb fell on Hiroshima, 1.6 km from her home. At twelve, in hospital with leukaemia, she set out to fold a thousand cranes, using medicine wrappers and any paper she could find. She folded more than a thousand before she died in October 1955. Her classmates raised a statue of her in the Hiroshima Peace Park, and every year about ten million paper cranes are sent there.

The oldest folding book

About 1600

A sword fitting made by Gotō Eijō is decorated with a folded paper crane, a sign that people were folding for play by then.

1797

Hiden Senbazuru Orikata, “The secret of folding a thousand cranes”, the oldest known technical book on origami for play: 49 designs by the monk Gidō of Ise Province, with comic poems by Akisato Ritō.

Renzuru

Many of Gidō’s designs are joined cranes, folded from one sheet with careful cuts so that the birds stay linked at a wingtip or beak. Kuwana City made his 49 methods Intangible Cultural Properties in 1976.

The 20th century

Akira Yoshizawa created the system of arrows and dashed lines for origami diagrams that Randlett and Harbin later improved. Diagrams still use it today.
