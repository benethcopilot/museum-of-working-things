Numbers & Elements · 1622–1976

Slide Rule

For three hundred and fifty years engineers multiplied by sliding one ruler along another. Bridges, jet engines and the trip to the Moon were worked out this way, to about three figures, with the decimal point kept in their heads.

Slide Cursor

Drag the middle strip to move the slide, and drag the glass to move the hairline. With the rule selected, the arrow keys move the hairline and Shift with the arrows moves the slide.

    Why sliding multiplies

    On the D scale, each number sits at a distance from the left end equal to its logarithm. The logarithm of 2 times 3 is the logarithm of 2 plus the logarithm of 3. Sliding C along D adds those two lengths, so the hairline lands on 6.

    The rule never shows where the decimal point goes. 2 × 3, 20 × 3 and 0.2 × 0.3 all land on the same spot. Engineers estimated the size of every answer in their heads first, which kept them in close touch with their numbers.

    Three and a half centuries

    1. 1614

      John Napier publishes his logarithms, which turn multiplying into adding.

    2. 1620

      Edmund Gunter of Oxford draws a logarithmic scale on a single rule. With a pair of dividers it can multiply and divide.

    3. c. 1622

      William Oughtred of Cambridge slides two of Gunter’s scales against each other and makes the first slide rule.

    4. 1859

      Amédée Mannheim, a French artillery lieutenant, makes the C scale match D and does most of the work on them. Most later rules follow his layout.

    5. 1969

      Aluminium Pickett slide rules fly on the Apollo missions. Buzz Aldrin’s N600-ES went to the Moon with him on Apollo 11.

    6. 1972

      The HP-35, the first handheld scientific calculator, goes on sale for $395. By 1976 the TI-30 costs under $25, and slide rules disappear from desks within a few years.

    7. 1980

      Texas schools hold their last slide rule contest in the University Interscholastic League.
