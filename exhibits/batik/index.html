Javanese Batik

Batik makers draw on cotton with hot wax. Where the wax goes the dye cannot, so the cloth keeps its colour there. Stamp the wax with a copper cap, write it with a canting, dip the cloth in indigo, scrape and wax again, dip it in soga brown, then boil the wax away.

Batik tulis, written

Wax is written with a canting: a small copper cup with a spout on a wooden handle. It makes the finest patterns, and the slowest.

Batik cap, stamped

A copper stamp prints wax over a whole area at once. Its pattern is built from copper strips bent and set on edge: the lightest for fine outlines, heavier for lines, the heaviest for the frame.

Batik lukis, painted

Wax is brushed on, which is useful for covering large areas, and dyes can be painted too.

Whichever tool is used, the cloth is dipped, dried, waxed again for the next colour and dipped again, as many times as there are colours. At the end the wax is removed by boiling or scraping. On 2 October 2009 UNESCO recognised Indonesian written and stamped batik as part of the intangible heritage of humanity, and Indonesia now keeps 2 October as Batik Day.

Forbidden patterns

Some patterns of the Javanese courts were batik larang, “forbidden batik”, worn only by the ruler and his family.

Kawung

From Yogyakarta: ovals in a grid like the fruit of the kawung palm, or a lotus with four petals. A sign of authority and justice, once used only by the royal family.

Parang

“Slope”: bands of S shapes running down at 45 degrees. Also batik larang; the size of the parang showed the wearer’s rank in the royal family.

Truntum

Small stars, created by Kanjeng Ratu Kencana, queen of Paku Buwana III (1749–1799), as a sign of love that guides and grows.

Indigo, then soga

Court batik from Yogyakarta and Solo was made in deep indigo blue and the browns of soga, a dye from the trunk of the soga tree. A museum record of the process: the design is waxed with a copper cap, the cloth is dyed in indigo, waxed again, and dyed in soga. Yogya sogan is mostly dark brown, black and white; Solo sogan is orange-brown and brown.

“Unintentional crackling is a sign of poor craftsmanship.” Yet “a crackle effect can be produced all over the design by crushing the fabric … just before the last dipping.”Pieter Mijer, Batiks and How to Make Them, 1919

Mijer explains why: pure beeswax cracks very little, and the more paraffin in the wax, the more it crackles.
