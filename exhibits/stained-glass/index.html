Stained Glass Window

A stained-glass window is a puzzle of coloured glass held together by strips of lead, and it is dull and dark until light comes through it. Choose the glass for each piece, stain it, paint it, and then let the sun come round and throw your colours across the chapel floor.

From cartoon to cathedral

  1. The cartoon

    A full-size drawing of each opening, in the Middle Ages drawn straight onto a whitewashed table, then divided into a pattern for every piece, with the lead lines marked.

  2. Cut and groze

    Each piece is chosen for its colour and cut to the pattern, its edges trimmed exactly with a grozing tool that nibbles away small bits.

  3. Paint and fire

    Faces, hair and folds are painted on in glass paint, ground lead or copper and glass in gum arabic, then fired in a kiln so they fuse to the glass.

  4. Lead and cement

    The pieces slot into H-shaped lead strips, cames; every joint is soldered, and an oily cement is pushed in to stop rattles and keep out the rain.

Colour made in the pot

Most coloured glass is pot metal: metal oxides stirred into the molten glass so the colour runs all the way through.

Cobalt, at just 0.025 to 0.1 percent, gives the brilliant blue of Chartres Cathedral.
Red is so intense that glass thick enough to stand the wind would look black, so a thin red layer is flashed onto clear glass. Abrade through it and the clear shows.
Manganese gives amethyst purple, one of the oldest glass colours of all.
From about 1300, silver stain painted on and fired gives yellows from pale lemon to deep orange, and turns blue glass green.

Light for those who could not read

In the Middle Ages stained glass became one of the great pictorial arts, telling the stories of the Bible to people who could not read. Gothic builders opened their walls to it, and the round rose window grew from simple pierced stone into vast wheels of glass.

  1. 675

    Benedict Biscop brings glaziers from France for his monastery at Monkwearmouth, the earliest reference to church windows in Britain.

  2. 1200s

    The windows of Chartres Cathedral, and the glass of the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris.

  3. 1313

    The earliest dated window using silver stain, at Le Mesnil-Villeman in Normandy.

  4. c. 1450

    “Cousin’s rose”, a fired red, is used for flesh tones.

  5. 1880

    John La Farge patents opalescent glass in February; Louis Comfort Tiffany patents his own versions that November.
