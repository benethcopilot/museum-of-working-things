Numbers & Elements · Padua, about 1604

Galileo’s Ramp

Things fall too fast to time with a 17th-century clock, so Galileo slowed falling down: he rolled a bronze ball along a grooved ramp. Put seven thin frets across the groove so the ball clicks over them in a perfectly even rhythm, then look at where the frets ended up.

Where the frets are

Distances are measured down the ramp from where the ball starts. The last column divides each gap by the first gap.

The odd numbers

When the clicks come evenly, the gaps between frets grow as

In each equal stretch of time the ball covers 1, then 3, then 5, then 7 times the first distance. Add them up and the distance from the start runs 1, 4, 9, 16: the squares of the times.

Tilt the ramp more or less and everything speeds up or slows down, but the pattern stays the same. Galileo argued that a falling body is the same story told faster.

A musician’s son

  1. 1591

    Vincenzo Galilei dies. A lutenist and music theorist, he had tested how the pitch of a string changes with its tension. His son grew up with an ear trained to keep time.

  2. 1604

    Working in Padua, Galileo arrives at the rule that a falling body covers distances that grow as the squares of the times.

  3. 1638

    His Two New Sciences, printed in Leiden, describes the ramp: a wooden moulding 12 cubits long, a groove lined with smooth parchment, a hard, smooth bronze ball, and one end raised one or two cubits. He timed each run by weighing the water that ran from a large vessel through a thin pipe.

  4. 1961

    Thomas Settle rebuilds the experiment and shows that a water clock is accurate enough to find the law.

  5. 1970

    Historian Stillman Drake suggests Galileo may first have found it by ear, moving frets until the clicks matched a steady beat, as this exhibit lets you do. It is a reconstruction; Galileo never described doing it.

Why a ramp?

A ball dropped from a tower falls 5 metres in its first second. Nobody in 1604 could time that. On a gentle ramp the same motion is slowed down many times over, slow enough to count, but it keeps its shape.

With the top raised one cubit, the ball here takes about 4.3 seconds to roll all twelve cubits, so the beat comes roughly every half second.

A rolling ball goes a little slower than one sliding without friction, because some of its energy goes into spinning. The pattern of odd numbers is unchanged.
