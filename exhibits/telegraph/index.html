Machines & Messages · 1844–2006

Telegraph Office

For a century, the fastest way to send news was a brass key, a wire and an operator who could hear letters in clicks. Tap out Morse code yourself, then write a telegram, pay for it by the word and send it down the line.

What you’ve sent

Hold the key, or hold Space. A quick tap is a dot, a longer press is a dash. Pause between letters and a little longer between words.

At the other end

Press send and the receiving operator copies each letter as it clicks in.

The Wrights’ telegram arrived with two mistakes made along the wire: the flight lasted 59 seconds, not 57, and Orville’s name came through as “Orevelle”.

Copy by ear

Operators didn’t count dots. They heard each letter as a rhythm, the way you hear a word. Listen and type what you hear.

The words are ones that turn up in telegrams again and again: arrive, money, home, train, safe. Start slow. When a letter sticks, tap it in the chart above and listen to it a few times.

Landline operators listened to a sounder. Its arm clicks down when the key closes and clacks up when it opens, and the time between the two is the dot or the dash. Radio operators, from the 1900s on, heard a tone instead.

A century on the wire

  1. 1844

    On May 24 Samuel Morse sends “What hath God wrought” from the Capitol in Washington to Alfred Vail at the railroad depot in Baltimore. The paper tape Vail received is in the Library of Congress.

  2. 1861

    The first transcontinental telegraph is finished on October 24. The Pony Express stops running two days later.

  3. 1865

    Europe adopts a simpler version of the code, which becomes International Morse. American railroads and telegraph companies keep Morse’s original, now called American Morse.

  4. 1895

    A Western Union officer works out that the average telegram, not counting newspaper dispatches, runs just under fourteen words. People went to great lengths to stay inside the cheap first ten.

  5. 1903

    The Wright brothers’ news goes from Kitty Hawk to Dayton on a key made by J. H. Bunnell & Co. That key is now at the Smithsonian’s National Air and Space Museum.

  6. 1912

    An international radio conference in London makes International Morse the code for radio messages.

  7. 2006

    Western Union sends its last telegrams in January. By then it handled about 20,000 a year, at around $10 each.

Two Morse codes

LetterAmericanInternational

American Morse had dashes of different lengths and gaps inside some letters. O was dot, space, dot. It was quick to send but easy to get wrong, and a gap inside a letter could be heard as two letters.
