Roman Mosaic Workshop

A Roman floor was made of thousands of small cut stones called tesserae, pressed one at a time into wet mortar. Paint the design on the mortar, choose how the stones will be laid, and watch the rows go down. The way they flow, which mosaicists call the andamento, is half the picture.

How a floor was laid

  1. Gather the stones

    Local stone, with cut brick, tile and broken pottery, gave mostly white, black, red, yellow and blue. Marble, glass and even gold were used now and then.

  2. Mark the design

    Guidelines were scored or painted onto the mortar bed. Some designs were pegged out with string.

  3. Lay the tesserae

    Fine pictures called emblemata were made in a workshop with tiny tesserae and set into the floor as its heart. The rest was laid on site in rows.

  4. Grout

    The gaps between the stones are filled, binding them into one hard surface to walk on for centuries.

The opus

Opus is Latin for “work”, and in mosaic it means the way the pieces are cut and placed. The movement and flow of the rows is the andamento.

Opus regulatum
A grid: every tessera lines up across and down.
Opus tessellatum
Rows across or down, but not both. The everyday Roman floor, laid on site.
Opus vermiculatum
“Worm-like work”: lines of tesserae follow the edge of a figure, one of the most demanding kinds. It reached Italy in the 1st century BC.
Opus musivum
Vermiculatum carried through the whole background.
Opus sectile
A whole shape cut from a single piece of stone.

Floors to walk on

Cave canem. “Beware of the dog” greets visitors in black and white tesserae at the door of the House of the Tragic Poet in Pompeii, 1st century AD. Petronius’ Satyricon describes the same words painted in big letters on the wall at Trimalchio’s dinner.

The Alexander Mosaic. Made around 120–100 BC for the House of the Faun in Pompeii, it shows Alexander the Great charging Darius III. It is 2.7 by 5.1 metres, in reds, yellows, black and white, with tesserae about 2 mm wide: an estimated one and a half million of them, perhaps more than four million.

Labyrinths. Fifty-seven Roman maze floors are known, many in bathhouses. Making your way through one was thought to bring good luck.

A cheaper Italian style used only black on white. At the other end, the hunting gallery of the Villa Romana del Casale in Sicily, about 300 AD, covers some 300 square metres.
