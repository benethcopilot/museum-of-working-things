Watercolour Washes

Watercolour is pigment held in gum arabic, thinned with water, and so transparent that the white of the paper does the work of white paint. The water decides as much as the painter: on damp paper colour runs and blooms, on dry paper it stops where the brush stops, and as a puddle dries it carries its pigment to the edge. What the water does

In a wash, diluted pigment is spread evenly across the paper, flat or graded from dark to light. Wet on wet, colour put onto damp paper spreads and blends softly; wet on dry, it keeps sharp edges. Because the paint is transparent, a glaze over dry colour lets the layer beneath show through, so blue over yellow reads as green.

Lifting takes pigment back off with a brush, sponge or cloth, though staining colours resist it. Salt sprinkled on a wash that is wet, but not too wet, soaks up water and pushes the pigment away, leaving pale blooms. Some pigments, ultramarine above all, have heavy grains that settle into the hollows of rough paper and leave a speckled texture called granulation.

The paint box

Until the late 1700s painters ground their own colours or bought hard, resinous blocks that had to be laboriously rubbed out in water. Around 1781 William and Thomas Reeves won the Society of Arts’ silver palette for a moist paint-cake that dissolved at the touch of a wet brush. Since the 19th century the binder has been gum arabic, with glycerin or honey added so the paint stays soluble.

The English made watercolour an art of its own. Paul Sandby is called the father of English watercolour, Thomas Girtin painted large romantic landscapes, and J. M. W. Turner took the medium to its height.

Older than any other paint

In its simplest form, earth and water, watercolour is as old as the cave paintings of the Upper Palaeolithic. Monks used it to brighten manuscripts. Albrecht Dürer painted his Young Hare in watercolour and bodycolour in 1502, but for centuries most painters kept it for sketches until the English school made it a medium for finished pictures.
