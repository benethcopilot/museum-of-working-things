Shibori Indigo

Shibori means “to wring, squeeze or press”. Before cloth goes into the indigo vat it is folded and clamped, bound with thread, wrapped round a pole or stitched and pulled tight, and wherever the cloth is squeezed the dye cannot reach. The pattern stays hidden until the threads are cut. Tie a cloth, dip it, and watch it turn blue in the air.

Ways of resisting the dye

Japanese dyers group the methods into three: kōkechi, cloth tied or bound; rōkechi, cloth protected with wax; and kyōkechi, cloth folded and clamped between carved boards. Within those there are hundreds of named techniques, often belonging to a single town or family.

Kanoko, “fawn spots”

Small points of cloth bound tight with thread, leaving rings like the spots on a young deer. This is what the West calls tie-dye.

Kumo, “spider”

Cloth pleated finely and bound along a cone, leaving a web-like burst.

Arashi, “storm”

Cloth wrapped diagonally round a pole, bound with thread and pushed down tight, leaving slanting lines like driving rain.

Nui, “stitched”

A running stitch pulled so tight, often with a wooden dowel, that the gathered cloth resists along the line.

Itajime, “board-squeezed”

Cloth folded several times and clamped between two shaped boards, so one shape repeats across the cloth when it is opened.

Green, then blue

Indigo does not dissolve in water, so the dyer first turns it into a soluble form, leucoindigo, which is yellow. Cloth comes out of the vat a yellowish green; as the air reaches it the leucoindigo turns back into blue indigo, now locked into the fibres. Every dip and airing adds another layer, so dyers build a dark blue slowly. A Babylonian tablet from the 7th century BC already describes dyeing wool lapis-blue by repeated dipping and airing.

In the Edo period indigo became the colour of ordinary Japanese clothing: textiles were booming, and commoners were forbidden to wear silk, so they wore cotton dyed blue.

Twelve hundred years of tied cloth

The oldest shibori to survive in Japan was given to the Tōdai-ji temple in Nara in 756, among the treasures of the late Emperor Shōmu, and already shows bound, waxed and clamped resists. Resist-dyed cloth from China is older still, from 418. The beni itajime of Kyoto, clamped between boards and dyed red with safflower, died out when the last workshop closed in 1932, and was only worked out again by researchers after 2010.
