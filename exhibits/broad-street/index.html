Life Science · Soho, London, 1854

Broad Street Pump

In the first days of September 1854, cholera killed hundreds of people in a few streets of Soho. Most doctors blamed bad air. A physician named John Snow marked every death on a map and asked a different question: where did these people get their water?

The deaths that didn’t fit

A few people died far from Broad Street. Snow went and asked.

“There were only ten deaths in houses situated decidedly nearer to another street-pump. In five of these cases the families of the deceased persons informed me that they always sent to the pump in Broad Street, as they preferred the water… In three other cases, the deceased were children who went to school near the pump in Broad Street.”

The brewery

The brewery on Broad Street stood next to the pump, yet none of its workers caught cholera. They were given a daily allowance of beer and drank no water from the well. Brewing boils the water, which kills what is in it.

Cases like this, where people who didn’t drink the water stayed well, are the evidence that convinces.

The handle

“I had an interview with the Board of Guardians of St. James’s parish, on the evening of Thursday, the 7th September… In consequence of what I said, the handle of the pump was removed on the following day.”

Snow himself said the outbreak was already falling, partly because so many people had fled. The chart shows it.

From bad air to bad water

  1. 1849

    John Snow publishes his first essay arguing that cholera spreads through something swallowed, not breathed.

  2. Aug 31, 1854

    New cases jump from a handful a day to 56 on August 31 and 143 on September 1. Within a week three quarters of the people living nearby have fled.

  3. Sep 8, 1854

    After Snow meets the parish Board of Guardians, the pump’s handle is removed.

  4. 1855

    Snow publishes his map in the second edition of On the Mode of Communication of Cholera. He also shows that houses supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall water company, which drew from the sewage-laden Thames, had about fourteen times the cholera deaths of houses supplied from cleaner water upriver.

  5. 1855

    The Reverend Henry Whitehead, a curate who first doubted Snow, traces the source: the well had been dug three feet from an old cesspit, and water from washing a sick baby’s nappies had drained into it.

  6. 1992

    A replica pump without a handle is set up near the original spot, now Broadwick Street.

Why it matters

The authorities put the handle back once the outbreak was over and kept blaming bad air. Snow did not live to see germ theory accepted. His method outlived the argument: count the cases, put them on a map, find what the sick have in common, and look hard at the ones who don’t fit.

That is still how outbreaks are traced, and it is why this map is called the founding moment of epidemiology.
