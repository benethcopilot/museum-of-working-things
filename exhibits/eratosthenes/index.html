Sky & Time · Alexandria, about 240 BC

Eratosthenes’ ShadowHow a librarian measured the whole Earth with two sticks

At noon on midsummer day, a stick at Syene in southern Egypt cast no shadow. At Alexandria, far to the north, a stick cast a short one. Eratosthenes worked out that the difference meant the ground curves, and by how much.

Work it out

The sunlight’s angle at Alexandria is the same as the angle between the two cities at the Earth’s centre. So the distance between them is the same fraction of the whole way round.

Do it yourself with a friend

You don’t need Syene. Two people, one north of the other, each measure a stick and its shadow at local noon on the same day. The difference in angle and the distance between you give the size of the Earth. Schools around the world do this on the equinoxes.

The man they called Beta

  1. c. 276 BC

    Eratosthenes is born in Cyrene, a Greek city in what is now Libya. He studies in Athens.

  2. Later

    He becomes chief librarian of the Library of Alexandria and tutor to the young prince who became Ptolemy IV.

  3. c. 240 BC

    He measures the Earth. His own book, On the Measurement of the Earth, is lost. The simple version told here comes from the astronomer Cleomedes, who gives 250,000 stadia. Pliny and others quote 252,000.

  4. 1st c. BC

    Posidonius tries another way, using how high the star Canopus rises at Rhodes and at Alexandria.

  5. 1492

    Columbus chooses a figure for the Earth about a quarter smaller than Eratosthenes’. With the larger one he would have known the land he reached could not be Asia.

  6. Today

    The Earth measures 40,008 km around through the poles. Depending on the stadion, Eratosthenes was within a few percent.

What the story leaves out

Cleomedes’ version assumes Syene sat exactly on the Tropic of Cancer and exactly south of Alexandria. Really it was a little north of the tropic and about 3 degrees of longitude east. Eratosthenes’ own method was more involved, and leaned on the surveys of professional pacers called bematists.

Critics nicknamed him Beta, “number two”, because he was second best at everything. Admirers called him Pentathlos, after the athletes who competed in five events. He also invented the sieve for finding prime numbers that carries his name.
