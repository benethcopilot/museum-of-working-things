Illuminated Initial

Strictly speaking, a manuscript is only “illuminated” if it holds metal, usually gold. Raise the letter in gesso, breathe on it, lay the gold leaf and burnish it until it flashes, then paint the ground in lapis blue and vermilion, in the order the illuminators worked.

EATUS VIR qui non abiit in consilio impiorum, et in via peccatorum non stetit, et in cathedra pestilentiæ non sedit: ¶ sed in lege Domini voluntas ejus, et in lege ejus meditabitur die ac nocte.

Psalm 1, “Blessed is the man…”. Psalters gave this B, the Beatus initial, their richest decoration.

Twelve steps to one letter

The text was written first, with a space left for the initial. The design was planned, often on wax tablets, and transferred to the vellum, sometimes by pricking holes along its lines. Then the illuminator worked through the page in a fixed order:

  1. Charcoal dust is dotted through to mark the outline
  2. A drawing in silverpoint
  3. The drawing is retraced in ink
  4. The surface is prepared for gold
  5. Gold leaf is laid down
  6. The gold is burnished to a mirror shine
  7. Decorations are pressed into the gold
  8. Base colours are painted
  9. Darker tones give volume
  10. Further details are drawn
  11. Lighter colours pick out particulars
  12. Ink outlines finish the work

Gold always comes before colour. Gold leaf will stick to any pigment already on the page, and burnishing is vigorous enough to smudge paint nearby. Try laying gold over a painted ground and see.

Gesso, breath and a tooth

Cennino Cennini, writing in Padua around 1400, gave the recipe for gilding on parchment: fine gesso, a little white lead and a little sugar candy, ground with water, dried out of the sun and tempered with beaten egg white. Laid thick, it stands up from the page, and the relief lets the gold catch light from every direction.

The gold may be laid “either with breathing or without breathing”, then burnished with a smooth, solid wooden panel under the parchment.Cennino Cennini, The Craftsman’s Handbook, chapter 157, in D. V. Thompson’s translation

For burnishing, Cennini lists hematite, precious stones, and the teeth of a dog or a wolf. From the 12th century gold leaf was usually burnished. Scraps of leaf that miss the letter were swept up and ground with honey into shell gold, a paint named after the sea shells painters mixed their colours in.

The illuminator’s colours

Ultramarine, from the stone lapis lazuli: rare, costly and kept for the robes of the Virgin Mary and important figures.

Azurite, a cheaper blue mineral, often used for initials and borders.

Vermilion, mercury sulfide, found as the mineral cinnabar.

Minium, red lead. Colouring with it was miniare, which gave us the word miniature.

Verdigris, made by boiling copper plates in vinegar. Green was rare on the page.

White lead, made by corroding lead with vinegar, for highlights and fine penwork.
