Litter Lab

Every puppy gets two copies of each gene, one from its mother and one from its father. A Punnett square lays out every way those copies can pair up, so you can predict a litter before it is born. Labrador Retrievers are perfect for practice: just two genes decide whether a Lab is black, chocolate or yellow.

The 1899 mystery

Two black Labradors, Neptune and Duchess, had a yellow puppy. His name was Ben of Hyde, and he is the first yellow Lab on record. How can two black dogs have a yellow puppy? Work through the steps, then solve it in step 3.

1

One gene: black or chocolate

The B gene decides whether a Lab’s dark color is black or brown. It comes in two versions. Big B makes black. Little b makes brown, which breeders call chocolate. One big B is enough to make a dog black, so B is dominant. A chocolate Lab must have two little b’s. Tap the letters on each parent to change their genes, then watch the square.

B gene · its scientific name is TYRP1

Each puppy gets one letter from Mom (down the side of the square) and one from Dad (across the top). Each box is one equally likely puppy.

2

Two genes: where yellow comes from

A second gene, E, decides whether that black or brown reaches the fur at all. With at least one big E, it does. With two little e’s, the fur can’t hold it and the dog is yellow, whatever its B gene says. When one gene hides another like this, scientists call it epistasis. The B gene still shows in the nose: a yellow Lab with a big B has a black nose, and one with bb has a brown nose.

E gene · its scientific name is MC1R

Each sperm or egg carries one copy of each gene, so a parent with Bb Ee can pass on four combinations: BE, Be, bE or be. That makes the square four by four: sixteen equally likely puppies.

3

Solve the 1899 mystery

Here are Neptune and Duchess, both black. With the genes they start with, no puppy of theirs could be yellow. Change their letters so a yellow puppy becomes possible, but keep both dogs black. They really were.

The rule you need

A yellow puppy needs two little e’s, and it can only get them if each parent gives one.

Detective challenge: Mystery Molly

Molly is a black Lab, but nobody knows which hidden letters she carries. To find out, breed her with Dudley, a yellow Lab with a brown nose. Dudley’s genes are bb ee, so he can only give b and e.     Mendel’s peas

    Gregor Mendel, a friar in Brno, crossed pea plants for about eight years and published his results in 1866. He showed that traits travel in pairs of units, one from each parent. Almost nobody noticed until 1900, when three scientists rediscovered his work.

    Punnett’s square

    Reginald Punnett, a geneticist at Cambridge, drew the square in his 1905 book Mendelism to make Mendel’s rules easy to see. Every cell is one equally likely pairing, so counting cells gives you the odds.

    Labs, in color

    Early Labradors were bred to be black. Liver puppies, now called chocolate, were recorded at the Duke of Buccleuch’s kennels around 1892, and Ben of Hyde, born in 1899, is the first yellow on record. The genes behind the colors were found about a century later: MC1R for yellow and TYRP1 for brown.
