Celtic Knotwork

Every knot in the Book of Kells begins as a simple plait. Cut two cords where they cross and join the loose ends the other way: that is a break. Click the crossings of the plait to make breaks, and watch a woven band turn into knots.

Plaits and breaks

The antiquary J. Romilly Allen explained the method in 1904. A panel’s sides are divided into equal parts and joined by diagonal lines, and the plait is drawn over that network, each cord passing over and under in turn.

“If now we desire to make a break in the plait any two of the cords are cut asunder at the point where they cross each other, leaving four loose ends … To make a break the loose ends are joined together in pairs. This can be done in two ways only.”J. Romilly Allen, Celtic Art in Pagan and Christian Times, 1904

Two horizontal and two vertical breaks side by side leave a space shaped like a cross. Allen found “eight elementary knots which form the basis of nearly all the interlaced patterns in Celtic decorative art”, two from a three-cord plait and six from a four-cord plait. He noted that plaits with an odd number of cords were seldom used, because they make lopsided patterns.

Every crossing on this page follows the rule of the plait: a cord that passes over at one crossing passes under at the next, all the way round, whatever breaks you make.

From Rome to Kells

3rd–4th c.

Plaits and interlace in Roman floor mosaics. Allen found no break in any Romano-British pavement.

514–523

The marble screen of San Clemente in Rome: still plaitwork without breaks.

712–795

True knotwork, with breaks, on dated church carvings in Lombard Italy: Valpolicella (712), Cividale (737), San Prassede in Rome (772–795).

7th c.

A Gospel fragment now at Durham Cathedral holds the earliest true knots in the Celtic manner.

c. 715–800

The Lindisfarne Gospels and the Book of Kells carry knotwork to its height, beside Welsh, Pictish and Irish stone crosses.

1951

George Bain’s Celtic Art: The Methods of Construction shows how to draw it again; his son Iain later simplified the method with grid lines.
