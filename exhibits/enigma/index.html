Break an intercept

Because the reflector sends the current back by a different wire, no letter can ever come out as itself. Codebreakers used that to place a guessed phrase, called a crib, in a message.

Intercept no. 180 lettersMade up for this exhibit, in the style of real traffic

Messages like this one often said Keine besonderen Ereignisse, “nothing to report”. Slide the crib along the message. A red square means a letter would have turned into itself, which Enigma can’t do, so the crib isn’t there.

Breaking it took Poles, Britons and one careless operator

  1. 1918

    German engineer Arthur Scherbius invents the Enigma at the end of the First World War and sells it to businesses. The German military adopts it in the 1920s and adds the plugboard.

  2. Dec 1932

    Marian Rejewski, a 27-year-old mathematician at the Polish Cipher Bureau, uses the theory of permutations to work out the wiring of the military rotors. With Henryk Zygalski and Jerzy Różycki he reads Enigma for years. He noticed that most messages began with ANX, German for “to” plus an X as a space.

  3. July 1939

    Five weeks before the war, at a meeting near Warsaw on 26 and 27 July, the Poles show French and British codebreakers what they have done and give each a reconstructed Enigma.

  4. Mar 1940

    The first British bombe, named Victory, arrives at Bletchley Park on 18 March. Alan Turing designed it and Harold “Doc” Keen of the British Tabulating Machine Company built it. It tested all 17,576 rotor positions against a crib.

  5. 1941

    Mavis Lever spotted an Italian message with no letter L in it. The operator had been told to send a dummy message and had pressed L over and over, so every letter except L could appear. The crib it gave helped lead to the British victory at Cape Matapan.

  6. Feb 1942

    The German Navy gives its U-boats a four-rotor Enigma, and Bletchley Park is shut out of their messages for many months.
