Shodō Brush and Ink

Shodō, the way of writing, is done with a soft brush, ink ground from a stick of soot, and absorbent paper that takes every stroke at once. Nothing can be corrected. Beginners practise one character, 永, “forever”, because it holds the eight basic strokes of regular script.

Slow strokes press the brush down and spread wide; fast ones lift it to a thin line. A brush that is running dry splits into streaks, called kasure. With a pen, pressure works too.

The eight principles of 永

Calligraphers since the Tang dynasty have taught the eight common strokes through 永. Each has an old name, a picture of how the brush should move. Select one to watch the brush write it.

The four treasures of the study

  • 筆BrushAnimal hair, often goat, sheep or horse, set in a bamboo or wooden handle.
  • 墨Ink stickPine or vegetable soot bound with glue and dried hard.
  • 硯InkstoneA stone slab with a well. Water is poured in and the stick ground against it.
  • 紙PaperThin and absorbent, laid on felt so the ink does not soak through, with a paperweight to hold it.

Grinding ink takes time, so many beginners now use bottled ink, bokujū. Students are also encouraged to carve their own seal, and the seal goes where it looks best, but never on a copied sutra.

Three ways to write

楷

Kaisho, regular script. Every stroke separate and complete, like printed capitals. The eight principles belong to this script.

行

Gyōsho, semi-cursive. Strokes run into one another as the hand speeds up.

草

Sōsho, cursive. Whole characters in a few flowing movements. The ensō circle is often drawn in this swift spirit.

Zen shaped Japanese calligraphy: the calligrapher has one chance at each sheet, and any lack of confidence shows. An ensō is drawn in one stroke and never changed afterwards. Some artists leave the circle open, some close it.
