Ink Bamboo 墨竹

Ink wash painting, called sumi-e in Japan, uses only black ink and water. A painter spends years on a few plants, and bamboo comes first: a stalk, its knots, its branches and its leaves, each laid down in one stroke that can never be taken back.

There is no undo, just as on real paper.

Stem, knot, branches, leaves

The Mustard Seed Garden Manual of Painting was printed in China from woodblocks, in colour. Its landscape volumes appeared in 1679, and Wang Gai and his two brothers added the volumes on plants and animals in 1701. Its Book of Bamboo opens with the rule this studio follows:

“The first principle of bamboo composition is, the four parts of the plant should be considered in the following order: stem, knot, branches and leaves. If the basic rule is not followed, time and effort will be wasted and the picture will never be completed.”Mustard Seed Garden Manual, Book of Bamboo, 1701

Generations learned from it. Qi Baishi started his lessons with the manual, and woodblock copies reached Edo Japan, where painters of the nanga school trained on it.

Whole bamboos in his heart

Bamboo bends in wind, storm and rain but never breaks, so it came to stand for a gentleman’s courage and endurance. Some painters painted nothing else all their lives, and painting ink bamboo was set as a subject in the imperial examinations.

Of Wen Tong, who painted bamboo around 1060, people said “there are whole bamboos in his heart” (胸有成竹). He held the plant complete in his mind before the brush touched the paper. The phrase is still used in Chinese for someone who has worked out a plan before starting.

The rule on the paper is strict: once a stroke is painted it cannot be changed or erased.

The Four Gentlemen

Bamboo is one of four plants that ink painters have painted as a set since the Song dynasty (960–1279). They stand for virtues such as uprightness, purity, humility and perseverance, and together they make a year.

梅Winter

Plum blossom, flowering in the cold.

蘭Spring

Orchid, for nobility and loyalty.

竹Summer

Bamboo, for dignity and service to others.

菊Autumn

Chrysanthemum, for long life.

The four treasures of the study

Brush, ink, paper, inkstone (筆 墨 紙 硯). The brush is bamboo with hair from goats, wolves, rabbits or deer, tapered to a fine point. The ink is a stick of pine soot and animal glue, ground with a few drops of water on the inkstone in circles until the black is as strong as you want. The paper is Chinese xuan or Japanese washi, so absorbent that every stroke shows.

In a master’s hand one stroke can run from deep black to silvery grey, because the ink load and the pressure change as the brush moves.

From the Tang to Sesshū

Ink wash painting began in Tang dynasty China (618–907) and flourished under the Song. Zen monks brought it to Japan in the 14th century, where it is called suibokuga or sumi-e, “ink picture”. Sesshū Tōyō (1420–1506), the great master of the Muromachi period, travelled to China in 1468–69 to study.

Empty paper matters as much as ink. Hasegawa Tōhaku’s Pine Trees screens, painted around 1595 and now a National Treasure of Japan, leave about 85 percent of the paper bare.
