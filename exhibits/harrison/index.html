Sky & Time · London, 1714–1773

Harrison’s Sea Watch

A sailor could find latitude from the Sun. Longitude, how far east or west, was a guess, and ships ran aground because of it. The answer turned out to be a clock good enough to keep home time across an ocean. A Yorkshire carpenter named John Harrison built it.

The voyage to Jamaica

In 1761 Harrison’s son William sailed to Jamaica on HMS Deptford with H4. The voyage took 81 days and 5 hours. Every second the watch gains or loses becomes a quarter of a minute of longitude. Choose a watch and see where you would think you were.

Four timekeepers and thirty years

Harrison spent three decades on large clocks for the sea before he realised a watch could do better.

H1

Finished in 1735: a big brass sea clock with two linked balances instead of a pendulum, so a ship’s rolling would not upset it.

H2 and H3

Larger and more refined, built over the next twenty years. H3 introduced the bimetallic strip to correct for temperature, still used in thermostats.

H4

A silver watch only about 5.2 inches across, beating five times a second, with pallets cut from diamond. It is the one that went to sea in 1761.

Longitude by the Moon

The rival method. Measure the Moon against the stars, then spend hours with printed tables. On the 1764 trial it was within about 30 miles; H4 was within 10.

The longitude prize

  1. 1707

    A British fleet, unsure of its position, wrecks on the Isles of Scilly. The disaster makes longitude a national priority.

  2. 1714

    Parliament’s Longitude Act offers £10,000 for a method good to one degree, £15,000 for two-thirds of a degree and £20,000 for half a degree.

  3. 1730

    Harrison, a self-taught clockmaker who began with wooden clocks of oak and lignum vitae, brings his first plans for a sea clock to London.

  4. 1761–62

    H4 sails to Jamaica on HMS Deptford. Allowing for its known daily rate, it ends 5 seconds slow after 81 days: about one nautical mile of longitude.

  5. 1764

    A second trial to Barbados on HMS Tartar. H4 is within 39 seconds. The Board of Longitude still suspects luck.

  6. 1773

    After years of dispute, and help from King George III, Parliament pays Harrison £8,750. In all he receives £23,065, though never the prize itself.

The arithmetic of a second

The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours: 15° an hour, 1° every 4 minutes, 1 minute of arc every 4 seconds.

At the equator a minute of arc of longitude is one nautical mile. Nearer the poles the lines of longitude draw together, so near Jamaica, at 18° north, it is about 0.95 of a mile.

A watch that loses a single minute a day would put you hundreds of miles out after a crossing. H4 had to stay within a couple of seconds a day, and its rate had to be steady enough to correct for.
