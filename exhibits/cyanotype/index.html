Cyanotype Sun Prints

In 1843 Anna Atkins laid seaweeds on paper brushed with iron salts, set them in the sun, and rinsed the sheets in water. Where light fell the paper turned a deep Prussian blue, and the plants stayed white. She bound hundreds of these prints into the first book ever illustrated with photographs. Make your own.

A finished print is on the board. Start a new sheet to make your own.

Five steps, one sheet

1Coat the paper

In dim light, brush the yellow-green sensitizer onto the sheet: iron salts that will react to sunlight. Paint with the pointer, or coat it all.

2Lay out specimens

Pick specimens from the tray and drag them into place. Select one and press Turn to rotate it. Drag it off the sheet to take it away.

3Expose to the sun

4Wash in cold water

The unexposed yellow salts dissolve and wash away. The blue stays.

5Dry

As the print dries, the blue darkens.

Make a test strip

Printers find the right exposure by covering a strip with a card and sliding it back every few minutes, so each band gets twice the light of the one before. Too little and the blue is weak. Too much and light soaks through the thinner parts of the specimen until its detail fills in.

What the sun does

John Herschel mixed two iron salts: ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide. Together they make a yellow-green coating that dissolves in water. Ultraviolet light in sunshine changes the iron in the citrate, and the changed iron reacts with the ferricyanide to make Prussian blue, a pigment that does not dissolve. Washing carries away everything the sun did not touch.

The paper only needs water to develop, so engineers and architects used the same process to copy drawings. From 1872 it was sold as “ferro-prussiate” paper, and until the 1940s most plans were copied this way. That is why a plan is still called a blueprint.

The first book of photographs

Anna Atkins grew up close to her father, the scientist John George Children. Both were friends of William Henry Fox Talbot, who taught her his early photographic methods, and of John Herschel, who invented the cyanotype in 1842. Within a year she had turned it on seaweed.

She meant her prints to illustrate William Harvey’s Manual of British Algae, which had no pictures. She made every plate by hand, one sheet in the sun at a time, and published the first part of Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions in October 1843, eight months before Talbot’s Pencil of Nature. She finished three volumes by 1853. Only 17 copies are known today.

  1. 1799

    Anna Children is born in Tonbridge, Kent, on 16 March.

  2. 1842

    John Herschel discovers and names the cyanotype. He never patents it.

  3. 1843

    Atkins publishes the first part of Photographs of British Algae in October.

  4. 1844

    The first part of Talbot’s The Pencil of Nature, the first photographically illustrated book sold commercially, follows in June.

  5. 1853

    She completes the third volume of algae, and with Anne Dixon makes Cyanotypes of British and Foreign Ferns.

  6. 1871

    Atkins dies on 9 June.

  7. 1872

    Marion and Company of Paris start selling cyanotype paper for copying plans.
