Split Rock Lighthouse

After the storm of November 1905 wrecked or damaged 29 ships on Lake Superior, the Lighthouse Service put a light on a 130-foot cliff north of Two Harbors, Minnesota. Its lens floated on a bath of mercury, turned by falling weights, and sent one white flash across the lake every ten seconds. Keep it turning.

Inside the lens

The lens is two great glass panels closed around the lamp like the halves of a clam shell, which is why it is called a bivalve. Rings of glass in the middle of each panel bend the light; prisms above and below catch the light going up or down and bounce it out level. Everything leaves flat, toward the horizon, where the ships are.

Why 22 miles?

The lake is curved. From high on the cliff the light can see farther over the bulge of water, and a sailor high on a deck can see farther back. Where the two horizons meet is as far as the lamp can reach, however bright it is.

The storm that built a lighthouse

On 27 November 1905 a storm swept east across the Great Lakes. On Lake Superior it brought easterly gales and heavy snow, with winds at Duluth reaching 68 miles an hour early on the 28th.

The steamer Mataafa, towing the barge James Nasmyth, turned back for Duluth. Trying to run between the piers of the ship canal she was thrown against them and grounded outside, where she broke in two. Nine men trapped in the stern died of cold in the night while people watched from shore.

Up the North Shore the steamer William Edenborn was driven aground near the Split Rock River, and her barge Madeira broke loose and was smashed against Gold Rock, close to where the lighthouse now stands. Seaman Fred Benson’s courage saved all but one of her crew.

By the count of the Minnesota Historical Society, the storm disabled or destroyed 29 ships and took 36 lives. The shore here had another danger: iron in its rocks pulled compass needles off true. Shipowners asked for a light, and in March 1907 Congress set aside $75,000 to build one.

  1. 1905

    The November gale damages or destroys 29 ships on Lake Superior.

  2. 1907

    Congress appropriates $75,000 for a light station between Two Harbors and Beaver Bay.

  3. 1909

    Work begins in June. There is no road, so every brick and beam arrives by boat and is hoisted up the cliff by a derrick.

  4. 1910

    The light shines for the first time that summer. Orren “Pete” Young is head keeper until 1928.

  5. 1924

    The North Shore highway reaches the station and tourists start arriving by car.

  6. 1940

    The station is electrified. A 1,000-watt bulb replaces the kerosene lamp and an electric motor turns the lens, so nobody has to wind the weights again.

  7. 1969

    Radar and radio navigation have made the light unnecessary, and it is retired.

  8. 1975

    The Edmund Fitzgerald sinks on 10 November with all 29 of her crew. Since 1985 the beacon has been lit every 10 November in their memory.

  9. 2011

    Split Rock Light Station is named a National Historic Landmark.
