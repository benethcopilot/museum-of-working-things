Jianzhi Window Flowers

Fold a square of red paper, cut through every layer at once, and open it out: one cut becomes two, four or eight, in perfect symmetry. Chinese families paste these cuttings, “flowers”, on their windows to welcome the spring and the new year. Fold, cut, and hang yours against the light.

What the cuts say

Most cuttings are called hua, “flowers”, meaning a pattern rather than a plant. Red paper is the favourite because red stands for happiness and good fortune, though any colour can be used.

福Fu

Good fortune, cut for the Chinese New Year.

囍Shuangxi

Double happiness, cut for weddings: two 喜, “joy”, side by side.

团花Tuanhua

Round flowers, folded to symmetry, standing for balance and wholeness.

Folding makes the symmetry. The paper is usually folded an even number of times, twice or four times, so that a single cut repeats around the centre when it is opened. Try cutting half of 囍 against the fold: the double happiness appears only when you unfold it.

Scissors or knife

With scissors, up to eight sheets are fastened together and cut at once with sharp, pointed blades. With a knife, the layers of paper lie on a soft bed of tallow and ash, and the craftsman carves the pattern, usually following a design but sometimes freehand.

The northern style

From Yuxian and Fengning in Hebei and from northern Shaanxi: bold, exaggerated shapes, animals and patterns, often simple and symmetrical. Yuxian cuttings are dyed in layers of bright colour.

The southern style

From Foshan in Guangdong and from Fujian: precise, decorative and elegant.

Miao cuttings

In southeast Guizhou, papercuts are not for windows but patterns for embroidery.

Older than most of the paper

Before paper, patterns were cut from gold and silver leaf, silk and leather. Paper made the craft cheap and everywhere. Because damp rots paper, few old cuttings survive in the rainy south, while the dry north kept more. In 2009 Chinese paper cutting was added to UNESCO’s list of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity.

    The court official Cai Lun is credited with inventing paper.

  2. 386–581

    The oldest known paper cutting, found in a grave in Xinjiang in 1959.

  3. 618–907

    Under the Tang, paper cutting becomes an art as well as a craft.

  4. 960–1368

    From the Song and Yuan, cuttings are pasted on windows to welcome the spring.

  5. 1368–1912

    Under the Ming and Qing, the art reaches its height.

  6. 2009

    Added to UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list.
